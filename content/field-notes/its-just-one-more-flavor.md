# It's Just One More Flavor

Every product meeting I have ever sat in has this moment. Somebody leans back and says, "It's just one more flavor."

And it sounds so reasonable. One recipe. One label. How hard could it be?

I used to answer that with a speech. (Nobody wants the speech.) So I built a picture instead.

![[field-notes/complexity-start.png]]

This is a plant on day one. One product, one size, one machine, one customer. Read it left to right: order the parts, receive them, store them, make the thing, package it, inspect it, store it again, pick it, pack it, ship it. Ten steps. One line running through them. Seven things on the shelf and four people to run the place. You could hold the whole operation in your head while making a sandwich.

Now the same ten steps, a few good years later.

![[field-notes/complexity-current.png]]

Nobody added a step. That's the part I want you to notice. It's the same plant doing the same ten things. What changed is that there are sixteen offerings now, and each one comes in several sizes, on four kinds of machine, in three kinds of package, sold five different ways.

Every thin line is one item somebody has to buy, make, count, find or ship. Sixteen offerings turned into 256 finished items. Those need 338 different things bought from 29 vendors. The warehouse has 768 places a picker might be sent. There are seven different ways out the door, each with its own paperwork and its own cut-off time.

Nobody holds that in their head. (People say they do. I have said I do.)

Which brings us back to the flavor.

In the tool there's a button that adds exactly one offering. Press it at this size and here is what arrives with it: 16 new finished items, 18 new things to buy, 48 new pick faces, and 19 more times per cycle that somebody stops a machine, cleans it out, and sets it up for the next thing.

One flavor.

I should be fair here, because it's my model and I know where it cheats. It counts high on purpose. It assumes every offering comes in every size, and a real plant skips some of those. It also knows nothing about volume. It counts how many different things there are, not how much of each you sell. The shape is right. The totals are a ceiling.

And none of this is an argument against new products. New products are the business. I have launched plenty and I'd do it again.

It's an argument for knowing what you ordered. The flavor is real, and so are the eighteen things behind it, and they all show up at receiving on the same day whether anyone drew the picture or not.

The model is generic. No company, no real products, nothing you could trace. If you run a plant you'll recognize yours anyway.
